**THE LIFESCIENCES MARKET**

* The life sciences market consists of human drugs & vaccines, medical devices, biologics, food\&cosmetics and animal drugs segments  
* A critical need for these companies is to stay on the right side of compliance by maintaining and implementing the right SOPs and quality processes in their manufacturing, clinical, laboratory, supply chain and production operations.   
* The US FDA regulates 30 cents out of every $ of GDP \- hence there is a critical market that needs to stay on the right side of compliance and regulations  
* The US FDA (and organizations like Health Canada and MHRA UK) conduct frequent inspections on the life sciences industry (and its suppliers) to ensure compliance. The process is as follows:   
  * There is an inspection by an FDA investigator  
  * If there is an issue, the investigator issues a Form 483 which has “observations” with issues that need remediation  
  * If the remediation is not satisfactory, the investigator issues a warning letter which means the company cannot sell its product or its raw material to any company in the US  
  * If they are still not satisfied, they issue an import alert which simply means the company cannot do any business in the US  
* 80% of drug shortage in the US is because of compliance issues \- hence the need to stay compliant is a critical one  
* Observations or adverse reports have critical consequences for companies. These include being barred from selling their products or doing business as well as negative impacts on company share prices. For this reason, companies spend upto 20% of their operating budget on quality assurance 

**THE CATEGORY**

* Given the market background, companies in the industry have various needs: to ensure they pass inspections, to keep track of whether their suppliers are in compliance, to keep track of how their competitors are doing (and any learnings), to maintain SOPs that don't have any gaps vs what investigators are looking for, to manufacture new products in line with current FDA guidelines   
* The basic data that they require to do so is the track record of all the investigations that are taking place across the world \- which investigators have inspected what sites at what companies and passed what observations.   
* FDA inspection observations are publicly available for companies to check and learn from. However, they are not available on time \- usually a lag of 3 months before a report is available. At best the FDA site may have around 250 letters at a time.   
* With the arrival of SaaS earlier and AI/ML now, a category has been created which can create access to this information. VCs call this category as “lifesciences compliance and regulatory technology”  
* There is also an allied category \- document management and workflow providers (like Veeva) which function like a github/google drive for company SOPs   
* The interesting dynamic though is that \- except for two providers, no one has thought of gathering and structuring reports for learning, either because they thought it was too hard to get and structure the data or because they haven't paid attention to this gap.   
* As a category (with different behaviors from different segments), one can hypothesize based on competitor usage that penetration is around 30-40% of the lifesciences (with exclusions like food/cosmetics etc as defined above) market. But this needs to be substantiated

* Our focus on the above market is only pharma/human drugs and vaccines, medical devices and biologics segments. Exclude the others

* Companies are extremely conservative in their outlook  
* This is a highly fragmented customer group with different behavioral traits as well as personalization needs. The likely segment groups and characteristics as we know them:

    * Innovators: top 50/100 companies which create the products. Usually US based \- companies like J\&J, BMS, Pfizer, Novo Nordisk etc.

India is largely a generics market \- e.g. Sun Pharma which is incidentally India’s largest and the 4th largest generics company in the world. Its the only Indian company in the top 100\)  
    * CDMOs \- these companies do contract manufacturing for a J\&J who will white label it and sell. These companies may not even have a drug license  
  * Segment level \- human drugs/pharma/vaccines, medical devices, biologics  
    * India is the pharma manufacturing hub while US based manufacturers are more into medical devices. (Interesting fact \- medical devices formed the highest number of inspections at 10000 companies  but the least proportion of 483 reports at around 800\)  
  * Size: enterprise (top 100\) vs mid market  
    * Not sure about this segmentation \- hypothesis only.   
    * Mid market relies on tribal knowledge \- human output \- build excel sheets  
    * They also hire ex FDA investigators as consultants who come in once a year.   
  * GxP level:   
    * Manufacturing vs clinical vs laboratory vs supply chain vs production  
    * Manufacturing stakes \- entire manufacturing is at stake if something goes wrong. For clinical, they are allowed to fail with the product but cannot fail with the process  
  * Geographic: Indian/chinese vs US  
    * Indian companies: more pharma manufacturing oriented   
      * Indian companies have upped their quality/compliance game over the last 10 years  
      * The Indian customer mentality is driven by “last minute prep” \- when an inspection is announced, they would like to quickly learn how to handle it. (In fact the FDA is now pressuring its investigators to come for inspections unannounced in order to counter this).   
      * The best time Indians want a product is when they get hit with an inspection or an adverse 483  
      * Indians are also cost conscious \- often company leadership directly handles the purchase \- so when they negotiate and save cost, they are saving money not only for the company but for themselves as well.   
      * Indian companies do not mind sharing their data with vendors (e.g. SOP uploads)   
      * They are quite comfortable getting messages or alerts on whatsapp.   
    * US companies: more medical devices/clinical oriented  
      * Loss of reputation is the most critical driver for them \- imagine Pfizer getting a warning letter.   
      * They also have suppliers doing the manufacturing in other countries which they white label. So compliance in their supply chain is a big worry for them  
      * They are more procedure oriented and all business is through emails  
      * They are more sensitive to confidentiality of their data   
      * If they like the product, they either route purchase through procurement or give a clear brief on the budget they have

If they are not satisfied, they officially mandate action to be taken. If they are still not satisfied, a warning letter goes to the CEO.
